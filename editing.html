Editing & Proofreading Services

Most authors arrive at editing with the same quiet fear that someone is about to take a manuscript they spent an incredible amount of time and effort creating and sand off everything that made it theirs. This page explains what editing actually involves, what each editing focus area does, and how much control stays with you.

Your Voice, Your Creativity

An editor’s job is not about making your book sound like a different book.

Your unique voice and creation are actually what we are protecting, but also nurturing and featuring. Editing is meant to find the places where what you meant did not survive the trip to the page and show them to you with suggestions and craft technique support to help you make well-informed decisions. It’s supposed to recognize the unique aspects that are your voice, your creativity, and make sure readers can see it too. Editing is meant to be a revision process that is collaboratively working toward delivering the reader experience you intended.

A sentence can be grammatically flawless and still fail. A sentence can break three rules and be exactly right. Voice lives in rhythm, in word choice, in the odd construction you chose on purpose, and an editor who flattens all of it into correctness has not improved your book. They have replaced it with a more obedient one.

The First Protection Is the Editor

Everything above is part of the working knowledge a properly trained, experienced editor who is passionate about written work should have, and why we are careful about who touches a manuscript.

An editor at that level is holding two things in balance on every page. On one side, your voice and your creative choices to be preserved and sharpened, not smoothed away. On the other, comprehension and ease of reading for the specific audience the book was written for, because a book no one can follow is not protecting your artistry either. The craft is in carrying both at once, and in knowing which way to lean when they pull against each other.

That balance is what you are actually buying. Not a proofread with a better name, and not a rulebook applied to your pages, but the judgment to tell a deliberate choice from an accident and to strengthen the first while correcting the second.

An editor without that judgment defaults to correctness because correctness is easy to defend. That is usually how books lose their voice, not to carelessness but to someone who had no framework for recognizing what was intentional or who preferred to take the easier road and not work as deeply into the creative and craft aspects.

The Second Protection Is That You Decide

Even with the right editor, you having the final say is the safety net, and it is absolute. Every edit arrives as a tracked change in Microsoft Word. Nothing is applied permanently to your document. Each suggestion sits alongside your original text, and you accept or reject them one at a time, or in whole passages, at your own pace. Where a change is not self-evident or there are multiple options, your editor leaves a margin comment explaining the reasoning and offering options.

If you have never used Track Changes, your Managing Editor will walk you through it before work begins, at no charge. It takes about twenty minutes.

We Show You Why, Not Just What

Editing is also a teaching opportunity, and we treat it as such deliberately. Where appropriate, margin comments carry the craft reasoning behind a suggestion and technique how-to information.

The immediate benefit is that you can make an informed decision about suggestions instead of guessing. The longer term benefit is that the same principles travel into your next manuscript and the one after that. Authors who engage with the teaching tend to need less editing over time—an odd thing for an editing company to work toward, and exactly what a partner should be working toward.

How much of this you want is up to you. Some authors want the full craft conversation. Others want the book fixed and would rather not discuss the theory. We take our cue from you.

Rules We Will Suggest Breaking

Our house standards are The Chicago Manual of Style and the Merriam-Webster Dictionary, and we work in both US and UK English. For nonfiction, we will also work to an additional style guide where your topic area calls for one.

Where your book departs from a standard on purpose—dialect, fragments used for rhythm, a genre convention, a punctuation habit that is doing real work—we do not correct it into compliance. We make note in an internal style sheet for your book that records the choice, then hold that line consistently across every page and every editing round. Inconsistency reads as error. A deliberate departure applied consistently reads as voice.

What We Will Push Back On

Protecting your voice is not the same as agreeing with everything. If a chapter is losing readers, if a timeline contradicts itself, if a scene is doing work you already did two chapters earlier, we will say so plainly and explain why.

You remain free to decline, but you should expect an editor who tells you the truth about the book. A service that agrees with everything is not doing you a service.

What Editing Actually Means

Editing is not one thing.

It is a set of distinct services addressing different levels of a book, from the shape of the whole manuscript down to the placement of a comma. Most books need two of them, not all of them, and knowing which is which is the difference between paying for what your book needs and paying for a package that serves a profit margin more so than quality results.

One thing to know before the definitions: three of these services mean different things depending on what you have written. What a developmental editor examines in a novel is not what they examine in a book meant to teach. Some books sit between the two—memoir especially, and personal or epistolary nonfiction—and for those we build a custom blend at the point of determining what the manuscript needs.

The services are listed from the largest scale to the smallest. Work moves in that direction, because there is no sense polishing sentences in a chapter that is about to be cut.

Ghostwriting

Not editing at all, though it is usually grouped with it. Ghostwriting produces a complete manuscript from your notes, documentation, recordings, and direction, written to your preferences and in your voice, for when the book exists clearly in your head or existing materials for other uses but not yet on the page.

You probably need this if you have the material, the expertise, or the story and writing it yourself is not going to happen.

Developmental Editing with Critique Report

Developmental editing coordinates and edits a project from proposal or rough manuscript through to a complete first draft, and it asks the highest-level question: does this book work? It is normally followed by line editing, which fine-tunes substantive and structural aspects at the line level.

It is available across the whole manuscript, or on a portion of it. The partial version—typically two to three chapters, or thirty to fifty pages—exists so an author can learn the patterns and apply them across the rest themselves to avoid the cost of a full developmental edit. You choose the section, or ask us to recommend one.

In fiction, the focus is on story

  • Whether the central problem or conflict is introduced early and whether it is relatable
  • Whether the resolution makes sense and is believable
  • Plot and pacing: whether the rise and fall of action lets a reader see and feel what is happening
  • Plot holes, inconsistent character behavior, and coincidences used to push the plot along
  • Point of view and transitions between scenes, chapters, and viewpoints
  • Character development and the balance between main and secondary characters
  • Theme: whether the experience you are trying to deliver actually lands
  • Clichés, vague imagery, info-dumping, under-explaining, repetition, and skipped ground

In nonfiction, the focus is on delivery

  • Whether the material teaches or persuades in a way that leaves a reader genuinely changed
  • Whether each paragraph builds toward the promise of its section, each section toward the promise of its chapter, and each chapter toward the promise of the title
  • Whether the building blocks are in the right order, without repetition or overwriting
  • Whether the elements of a working teaching tool are present and setup in a way that earns the reader’s buy-in, a clear theme, supporting content ordered to build an argument from the ground up, and key takeaways that state what the reader should have learned

What you receive

A written report covering a synopsis of the manuscript as it currently stands, its real strengths, its areas of opportunity, and craft guidance tied to specific margin notes in your manuscript, so you are not just told what to change but also shown how and why. A sample report is available on request.

You probably need this if the manuscript is not finished and you are unsure how to finish it, or it is finished and something is wrong that you cannot name.

Substantive / Structural Editing

For a complete manuscript that needs development work in its substance and structure before any technical correction would make sense. When a book needs that much moving, expanding, and cutting, correcting grammar alongside it is wasted effort—half of what was corrected is about to be rewritten or removed.

This is the least common service needed, and we would rather say so than sell it. Most manuscripts in this condition need developmental editing instead. Most of the rest have a volume of structural work light enough to be handled alongside copy editing in the same pass, which is Line Editing.

Line Editing

Substantive/structural editing and copy editing combined, delivered in one pass. Line Editing clarifies structure and fine-tunes content at the paragraph, line, and instance level to deliver the experience you intended, while keeping your voice and authenticity intact.

This is the most common plan for a well-developed manuscript needing some attention to substance and structure, plus paragraph-to-sentence-level technical correction, with the need for either inside a range where doing both at once allows thorough results.

In fiction, that means:

  • Cutting clichéd language, unclear imagery, info-dumping, thin explanation, and repetition, tailored to the conventions of your genre
  • Sentence structure, word choice, and precision: awkward phrasing, run-ons, ineffective fragments, monotonous sentence rhythm, vagueness, redundancy
  • Pacing and rhythm: paragraphs that drag or rush, dialogue that does not sound like a person, prose rhythm matched to the tone of the scene
  • Clarity: confusing passages, unclear pronoun references, sentences that need a second reading
  • Show versus tell: exposition that could become a scene a reader can feel
  • Point of view: unintentional perspective shifts and head-hopping without clear transitions
  • Voice and style consistency held steady throughout and aligned to genre and audience

In nonfiction, that means:

  • Considering the book’s concept and intended use, its content, organization, design, and style and making it functional for its readers, not merely correct
  • Whether it holds together as a coherent whole and the order of presentation is logical from the reader’s point of view rather than the writer’s
  • Whether everything necessary is present and everything unnecessary is gone
  • Whether the retrieval aids—table of contents, internal headings, appendices—are actually useful and use terms your audience would look for
  • Fine-tuning the elements of a teaching tool: setup, theme, supporting content, stories, key takeaways, and the wrap-up and segue that connects each chapter to the next

And in both:

Copy editing is included—grammar, word choice, sentence structure, paragraphing, and the mechanics of style, plus consistency of mechanics and internal consistency of facts. The point throughout is to polish and amplify your voice, not replace it, so readers move through the book without being pulled out by a clunky sentence or an unclear passage.

You probably need this if the book is well developed and you want it to read the way you heard it in your head. This is the editing type most manuscripts need.

Copy Editing

Rule-based technical accuracy at the paragraph, sentence, and word-choice levels. Grammar, word choice, sentence structure, paragraphing, and the mechanics of style, plus consistency of mechanics and internal consistency of facts, with strict adherence to the style guide you choose, guaranteed. There is some subjective judgment about pacing and reading rhythm, but only as it follows from technical changes to sentence structure and wording.

Copy Editing is rarely the right service on its own. It fits a well-developed manuscript carrying a high volume of technical correction and little else. For most books, Line Editing followed by a final Proofread is the better plan.

You probably need this if the writing is exactly where you want it and only the mechanics need work, beginning at the paragraph level rather than the word level Proofreading would cover. When this is the case, Copy Editing and Proofreading can often be combined to work all technicals, paragraph through keystroke, in one pass.

Proofreading

The final polish on your digital Word manuscript. Spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and mechanical errors at the word and keystroke level. Strictly rule-based, though some of those rules can be set by you, with adherence to The Chicago Manual of Style and the Merriam-Webster Dictionary guaranteed.

Proofreading happens before interior design, and that order matters. Once a manuscript moves into design software, the text is locked: a designer changes how the content looks, never what it says, and no keystrokes are altered after that point.

You probably need this if all other editing is completed or wasn’t needed. Every book gets one. Proofreading is not where a book gets better—it is where a finished book gets clean, and it will not rescue a manuscript that skipped the stages before it.

What Most Books Actually Need

For most manuscripts, the answer is Line Editing followed by a final Proofread.

Not five stages, not a package, and not whatever produces the largest invoice. Two services, in that order, is the most common correct plan.

Books arriving less finished than that generally need Developmental Editing first, and books arriving more finished than that sometimes need only Proofreading. Substantive/Structural Editing on its own is rare, and standalone Copy Editing rarer still.

There is a reason to care about this beyond cost. The stages are not independent. Good developmental work removes the need for structural work later; a thorough line edit removes most of what a separate copy edit would catch. Scoping correctly at the start is how the total cost comes down rather than go up.

If your manuscript does not need a stage, you will not be quoted for it, and if the work you do between assessment and start date removes the need for one, we will requote rather than hold you to the original higher cost.

The teaching approach works the same way over a longer horizon. What you learn during the editing process is what you already know while writing the next one, which is why authors who have been through the process with us usually return with a later book needing less work than the first.

How It Works

Assessment and sample edit first, then a plan you approve, then work delivered in stages with your review built into each one.

Nothing moves forward until you have signed off on what came before.

  1. Assessment. A Managing Editor reads into your manuscript, sample edits 1–2 pages, and produces a detailed written assessment of what it needs and why.
  2. The plan. You receive the recommended services with what each one will address in your specific book and what it costs. We fine tune as needed together, to finalize the plan before any work begins.
  3. Editing, stage by stage. Each stage arrives as a tracked-changes file with margin comments and a report where the service includes one. Track Changes training and support is included in your editing services.
  4. Your review. Nothing advances until you have worked through the previous stage and approved it. Your questions go to your Managing Editor, who has read in on your book from the beginning.
  5. Final Proofread. The last pass before the manuscript goes to interior design and the text is locked.

We do not cap revision rounds. You get as many passes as the book needs within the agreed scope, because a number written into a contract has a way of becoming a ceiling at the exact moment a book needs one more look.

Scope does have an edge: it ends where revision stops being about the manuscript, where the same ground gets covered again without the book moving, or where author revisions introduce more issues than they resolve. That is rare, and we will raise it directly and discuss how best to solve it.

Who Does the Work

The Pro Book Editor, our in-house editorial team.

Your editor is chosen for fit—the service your book needs, your genre or topic area, and your writing style—and a Managing Editor stays with your project from the first edit to the final proofread.

Why You Work With a Managing Editor Rather Than the Editor

This is a deliberate structure with several benefits. Your Managing Editor is read in on your book from the beginning and stays through the whole project. That puts one person in a position to do four things at once: check the quality of the work before it reaches you, manage the schedule, answer your questions during editing with full context, and carry what they have learned about your book forward into design and marketing.

That last one matters more than it sounds. The most experienced person in all things publishing and marketing, over an editor who typically specializes there, is the same person briefing your cover designer and shaping your book description. Nothing about your book has to be re-explained to anyone downstream, so nothing gets lost in a handoff and everything is based on a deep understanding of the material and your goals.

It also means your editor is editing rather than managing a client relationship, which is what you are actually paying them to do. If they are available to every author, then their work for the next author is always interrupted, and this kind of work requires long periods of intense focus.

If a question ever needs your editor specifically, your Managing Editor will get you that answer. In practice it rarely comes up. Your Managing Editor knows every detail of your project, and our editors work to the same house style guide and the same teaching method, so what comes back is what your editor would have told you, with the added context of where your book is headed in design and marketing, which your editor does not carry.

Human Editors, and Exactly Where Software Fits

Your book is edited by highly trained, experienced humans. Our editors do not use AI. No generative AI writes, rewrites, or edits your manuscript at any stage.

The single exception is a consistency checker called PerfectIt, which our Proofreaders run in addition to—never instead of—their own full read-through. It flags inconsistent hyphenation, capitalization, abbreviation, and departures from The Chicago Manual of Style across an entire manuscript. It does not generate text and does not rewrite sentences. It catches what a human eye reliably misses across ninety thousand words, and a Proofreader decides what to do with each instance it surfaces. It’s simply a tool that presents all the instances so none slip through the cracks of human imperfection.

Away from the manuscript, our Managing Editors and Marketing Specialists use AI for research and for developing categories, keywords, and marketing copy. Every result is reviewed and finalized by a person. Both tools run on company-held accounts configured so nothing uploaded can be used for AI training or shared beyond that account. Your manuscript is not training data, and it does not leave our control.
